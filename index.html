Generating Expressive Timing by Combining Rhythmic Categories and Lindenmayer Systems

Carlos Vaquero Patricio  &  Henkjan Honing
Institute for Logic, Language and Computation · Amsterdam Brain and Cognition · University of Amsterdam
Correspondence: c.vaqueropatricio@uva.nl
Abstract

This paper introduces a novel approach for modelling expressive timing performance by combining cognitive, symbolic, and graphic representations of rhythm spaces with Lindenmayer systems, originally conceived to model the evolution of biological cell structures and plant growth. Logo-turtle abstractions are proposed to generate expressive rhythmic performances defined by rule-based displacements through perceptual rhythm categories.

1.Introduction

In music performance, expressiveness arises from how performers deviate from a literal interpretation. Expressive parameters vary considerably between performers, even within the same piece [2]. We define expressiveness as the deviation from the most frequently heard version of a constituent musical element — an intrinsic definition, independent of a notated score [8, 11].

Memory and expectation play a fundamental role in recognising timing nuances [14, 15, 17]. Performers use non-linear models for features such as ritardandi, producing different expressive renditions each time [13]. Any computational model must therefore incorporate non-linear variation within perceptually constrained bounds. We propose combining Desain & Honing’s empirical rhythm categories with Lindenmayer systems and logo-turtle abstractions as an exploratory tool for computational music generation.

2.Rhythm Categories and Expressive Timing

Rhythmic perceptual categories are the mental clusters by which listeners relate continuous timing to discrete symbolic patterns [5, 15]. Desain & Honing [10] conducted a perceptual experiment in which musically trained participants notated, in music notation, all possible combinations of four-onset rhythms within one second (60 BPM). The resulting data defines a chronotopological map: a triangular space where each point is a rhythm of three inter-onset intervals (I₁, I₂, I₃) summing to 1 s. Modal centroids (•) correspond to H = 0; category boundaries to H = 1.

Crucially, the most frequently identified rendition (modal centroid) does not coincide with the integer-ratio metronomic position (×) — the latter may in fact be perceived as expressive [15].

3.Lindenmayer Systems

L-systems [21] model structure development via parallel string-rewriting. A grammar \(G = (N, S, \omega, p)\) consists of non-terminals \(N\), terminals \(S\), an initial axiom \(\omega\), and production rules \(p\) applied simultaneously to all symbols. In musical L-systems [23] three rule types are distinguished: production rules (structural rewriting), decomposition rules (unwrapping compound symbols), and interpretation rules (mapping symbols to musical parameters).

Prusinkiewicz [30] extended L-systems with a logo-turtle: state \((x, y, \alpha)\), step \(d\), rotation \(\delta\). Symbol F draws a forward step; + / - rotate. In the present model the turtle navigates the perceptual rhythm space rather than Euclidean geometry.

4.Generating Expressiveness with L-Systems

Each rhythm-category symbol (a–l) is an L-system token; production rules determine which categories are visited and in what order. Within each category, the turtle traces a path through an elliptical approximation of the perceptual boundary. Step size encodes the magnitude of expressive deviation; entropy \(H\) increases from 0 at the modal centroid toward 1 at the boundary.

Style-appropriate constraints can be encoded directly in the interpretation rules: small steps for music with little rubato, large steps for highly expressive styles.

5.Open Challenges

Tempo scaling. Sadakata et al. [32] suggest timing deviations are tempo-invariant, which may permit morphological inference of category boundaries at untested tempos. Cross-bar continuity. Expressive deviations must be consistent across bars; “mirroring” turtle positions across category boundaries maintains coherence. Fourth onset. A weighting scheme [29] can infer the fourth inter-onset interval needed to advance to the next bar.

6.Conclusions

We have presented a novel approach to modelling expressive timing performance by combining empirically derived rhythmic categories with Lindenmayer systems and logo-turtle abstractions. The model is perceptually grounded, intrinsically defined with respect to a score, and capable of producing non-linear expressive variation. Initial results are promising for automatic music performance systems, pending formal evaluation.
